[OminiControl2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.08280) targets the efficiency bottleneck of multi-condition control in diffusion transformers. These notes don't retell the paper; they answer the two questions I actually care about: **where the cost comes from**, and **what the evaluation does and does not prove**.

## Where the cost comes from

Universal image conditioning (the approach OminiControl pioneered) is flexible: encode each condition image into tokens and concatenate them into the DiT sequence, where they participate in attention. The price has two layers:

- Attention is quadratic in sequence length, and every added condition makes the sequence longer;
- Condition tokens get **re-processed at every denoising step**, even though they don't change across timesteps.

So conditional overhead balloons with both the number of conditions and the number of steps. OminiControl2's two fixes map one-to-one onto those layers: **compact token representation** (compress condition tokens, shorten the sequence) and **feature reuse** (compute condition features once and reuse them across denoising steps).

## How it proves efficiency

This is the most solid part of the paper — the evaluation spec is worth copying wholesale as a template:

- **Tasks**: Canny-to-image, depth-to-image, mask inpainting, image deblurring
- **Data**: trained on Text-to-Image-2M; evaluated on COCO2017 validation (5,000 images)
- **Quality metrics**: FID, CLIP-Text, CLIP-Image, NIQE, MUSIQ
- **Efficiency metrics**: inference latency, condition overhead (seconds / percentage), parameter overhead
- **Baselines**: OminiControl, SD 1.5 + ControlNet, FLUX + ControlNet variants, token merging, naive feature caching; ablations isolate feature-reuse-only and compact-representation-only
- **Training**: FLUX.1 + LoRA, Prodigy optimizer, 25k iterations per task

The headline number: in the multi-condition setting, conditional overhead drops from **24.38s to 0.93s** (~96.2% reduction) while FID stays competitive. Notably, speedup is reported separately along three axes — resolution, denoising steps, and number of conditions. That habit of reporting efficiency along each scaling axis independently is itself worth stealing.

<Callout emoji="🔍">
  One question the evaluation left open for me: does "multi-condition" mean four *different*
  condition types given simultaneously, or *more instances* of the same condition type? Those
  two settings differ completely in difficulty and meaning, and the paper never draws the
  boundary sharply.
</Callout>

## What it doesn't prove

FID / CLIP / NIQE / MUSIQ establish that generation quality holds up — but those are generic metrics. **There is no dedicated control-fidelity evaluation**: given a reference or condition, how faithful is the output to it? By contrast, work like MICo-Bench builds human-aligned, reference-based metrics specifically for multi-reference composition (e.g. Weighted-Ref-VIEScore, with human-verified reference images and face-source annotations).

The two evaluations are essentially orthogonal: OminiControl2 proves that **conditioning can be cheap**; MICo-Bench asks whether **conditioning is faithful**. A serious multi-condition benchmark probably needs both groups of axes — efficiency (latency / overhead / condition scaling) *and* fidelity (reference / identity / prompt alignment).
